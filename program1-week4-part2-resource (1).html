Adapt the guidance to your consultation

Shaping Our Lives wrote the guidance. This page helps you translate it into a learning-design consultation about your own research question. It is a companion to their work and to the conversation itself, not a replacement for either.

Before you begin: no single vantage point holds the whole of what a research question needs. The people who live the barrier you are naming should help shape it, and their knowledge should lead. The reason to bring the question to others is not to study them; it is that a consequential question is stronger with more than one informed hand on it.

Read the source first

Shaping Our Lives is a disabled people's user-led organisation. Their guidance on involvement and consultation is written by the people most often on the receiving end of it.

Guides on inclusive involvement & co-production → Top tips for co-production →

As you read their guidance, hold your own question in mind and answer for your context. There are no correct answers; each prompt is here to surface the places you do not yet have one.

On timingShaping Our Lives distinguishes co-production, where people are involved from the start through to evaluation, from feedback gathered only at the end.

Worth sitting with: involvement that begins after the decisions are made is consultation in name only, however sincerely it is offered.

On barriersTheir work notes that people who face inequalities face added barriers to taking part, and that barriers left unaddressed mean their perspectives go unheard.

Worth sitting with: what you leave unaddressed decides, quietly, whose knowledge shapes the work.

On reciprocityThey are clear that expertise should be valued and resourced: people inducted, supported, and paid, as with any contribution to the work.

Worth sitting with: expertise from lived experience is still expertise; naming how it is valued is part of treating it as such.

On the activity itselfThey treat the accessibility of an involvement activity as the organiser's responsibility, not something the participant should have to solve.

Worth sitting with: a conversation reaches only the people its format already fits.

On follow-throughThey advise acting on the suggestions people offer and reporting back on what happened; involvement without that loop wears people down.

Worth sitting with: being asked, and then hearing nothing back, quietly withdraws the invitation.

On what you ask aboutTheir guidance follows the social model: the barrier sits in the design and the environment, not in the person.

Worth sitting with: the design is yours to examine; a person's experience is theirs to share, if and how they choose.

Your answers stay on your device. Nothing is stored or sent.
