Pillar 1 · Infrastructure

Accessibility

The legal and technical floor that makes participation possible

What it is

Accessibility is the removal of physical, technological, and attitudinal barriers to learning. It is grounded in legal frameworks — the Accessible Canada Act, the ADA, the AODA — and technical standards like WCAG 2.1 (perceivable, operable, understandable, robust).

It is the floor, not the ceiling. Compliance establishes minimum conditions for participation, not equity of experience.

“Does this policy treat legal compliance as a starting point — or as the destination?”
What to look for

Retrieve your institution’s accessibility policy or a recent compliance report. Is the language reactive (“accommodations provided on request”) or proactive (“designed for access from the outset”)? Note whether it names specific standards or stays general.

Observation prompt

Locate one piece of content in your environment. Apply the WCAG lens: perceivable, operable, understandable, robust. Where does it pass? Where does it fail — and for whom?

Team prompt

Each person audits one piece of content independently, then compare. Where do your observations converge — and where do they differ? What does the difference reveal about whose experience of the system is most visible to your team?

Record what you found. Note the loudest signal, any surprises, and where the system passes or fails.

Pillar 2 · Ethics

Disability justice

The compass that asks who leads, who benefits, and who is still missing

What it is

Disability justice moves beyond the legal floor to a political question: who was in the room when this system was designed, and whose needs were written in as default? It centres intersectionality — a framework developed by legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw to describe how race, gender, class, disability, and other axes of identity compound one another, producing forms of exclusion that single-axis frameworks cannot see or address.

A core principle is the leadership of those most impacted. Inclusion designed without input from those excluded tends to replicate the conditions that created the gap.

“Do the people leading this program reflect the communities most affected by its gaps?”
What to look for

Look at who holds decision-making authority in the program you are auditing. Then look at who it serves. Apply an intersectional lens: are these the same people? Where are the power gaps? You are looking for evidence of whose perspective shapes the design.

Observation prompt

Locate one historically, persistently, or systemically marginalized (HPSM) group of learners in your current environment. What would need to change if their experience were the design brief — not an edge case?

Team prompt

Each person locates one HPSM group in your environment. Map where they overlap. Which exclusions were visible to everyone — and which required someone to locate them?

Record who leads, who is absent from the design process, and what that absence costs.

Pillar 3 · Pedagogy

Emergent learning

The method that keeps the system responsive

What it is

Emergent learning treats curriculum as a living system rather than a fixed delivery mechanism. Rooted in Freire’s critique of the “banking model” — where learners are passive recipients — it creates conditions for real-time adaptation, peer-based inquiry, and context-driven response.

Adaptation is not a sign of failure; it is skilled practice. Systems that can adapt are more resilient than those that cannot.

“Can you find one moment where someone responded to what was actually occurring — rather than following a fixed plan?”
What to look for

Observe a live or recorded session, or review a collaborative workspace. Look for genuine response: a facilitator who changed direction based on learner feedback, a peer reframe that shifted the group. If you cannot find one, that absence is a data point.

Observation prompt

Identify one moment where your environment responded to learners rather than delivered to them. What made that possible — and what would need to change for it to be the norm?

Team prompt

Each person shares one example of emergence they observed. Then ask collectively: were those conditions deliberate or accidental — and how do you build more of them?

Note where adaptation is happening, where the system is most rigid, and who bears the cost of that rigidity.

Map the silos

After completing all three pillars, consider where your environment integrates these elements and where they operate in isolation. The gaps are not failures — they are the starting point for your inquiry.

Pillars When integrated, this looks like… When siloed, this looks like…
Accessibility + Disability justice Legal standards informed by intersectional leadership; design that prevents exclusion rather than retrofitting it Technical compliance met without input from those most affected; standards that pass audits but fail people
Disability justice + Emergent learning Pedagogy shaped by the leadership of HPSM learners; real-time adaptation responding to their actual experience Critical frameworks articulated but not embedded in practice; values stated but not enacted
Accessibility + Emergent learning Technical infrastructure that adapts as the learning context shifts; access built in rather than added on Responsive pedagogy operating on inaccessible platforms; adaptive teaching inside inaccessible systems
All three together A learning system that is legally sound, ethically grounded, and pedagogically responsive — sustainable by design A gap between policy and practice that consistently drops the same learners
