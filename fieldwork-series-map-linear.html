Fieldwork — the series map

Linear, accessible version. Fieldwork is a research method for inclusive learning design, practised in public. It asks one question: in education, at work, in public service, how does learning design decide who is included? Twenty-four posts in four parts. Each one reads a single adjudicated decision from the public record through the same instrument, adds one finding to the tally, and hands the reader one entry to make from a setting of their own.

The question the series carries from the first post to the last: when a learning or working system was found to have failed someone, which of the three conditions could not act, and did anything in the outcome require the system to learn?

Three limits, stated here and returned to in Post 24. Adjudicated cases are the failures that were fought, so the corpus cannot see the ones that never reached a tribunal. Reading a judgment is not reading an institution. The series reads for learning design; it is not legal advice.

How to read this

This is the plain-text, screen-reader and keyboard-friendly version of the interactive series map. Nothing is hidden and nothing needs to be opened: every post appears in full, in order. Use your screen reader's heading navigation to move between parts (heading level 2) and posts (heading level 3).

Each post names five things:

Encounter
What the post opens on, and the claim it makes.
Decision
The adjudicated decision the post reads. Named only once it has been opened and read in full.
Make
The entry this post adds to the reader's own audit.
Afterwards
What can be done in a setting of one's own once the entry is made.
Finding
The post's one-line answer to the running question, added when the post publishes.

Part 1 — What was promised

A right declared, and what reached the person. Where a commitment on paper and a service delivered came apart, and what the record shows was decided in between.

Post 1 — Twenty-five Days

Encounter. A policy that was meant, and a learner it did not reach. A right declared and a right delivered are two documents, and the distance between them is where inclusion is decided.

Decision. Moore v. British Columbia (Education), 2012 SCC 61. Supreme Court of Canada; free to read.

Make. First entry: one declared document and one delivered document from a setting of your own, both dated, and who was consulted between them.

Afterwards. Find where a right declared and a right delivered have come apart, from documents a stranger could open.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the second, and the third was never engaged. The Court confirmed the discrimination and set aside the systemic orders; no order required the District to report.

Post 2 — 0.0025 Percent

Encounter. Three questions the hypothesis asks of any environment. Accessibility asks what the floor is. Disability justice asks who decided what it covers. Emergent learning asks whether it can move. Only the first can be answered once. This is the instrument the series applies twenty-three times.

Decision. Eldridge v. British Columbia (Attorney General), [1997] 3 SCR 624. Supreme Court of Canada; free to read.

Make. The three questions, in full: for each of the three conditions, the document that shows it able to act, or the blank where that document should be.

Afterwards. Tell, from an institution's own record, which of the three conditions is not able to act.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the second: the Ministry decided what the floor reached, twice, by refusing. The Court required the government to act and declined to say how; nothing in the outcome required a report.

Post 3 — The Help Number Was Not In Service

Encounter. A standard can be written, adopted and published and still never be applied. A system that had the rule and did not act on it, and what a court made of the distance.

Decision. Jodhan v. Canada (Attorney General), 2010 FC 1197; Canada (Attorney General) v. Jodhan, 2012 FCA 161. Federal Court and Federal Court of Appeal; free to read.

Make. Entry: one standard your setting has adopted, and the evidence it has been applied.

Afterwards. Separate a standard adopted from a standard applied, with dates for each.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the third, and the second was never exercised. The trial court required the system to report back; the appeal court removed that requirement.

Post 4 — Written Consent

Encounter. When a system and a family disagree about where a learner belongs, someone decides, and the record shows on whose account.

Decision. Eaton v. Brant County Board of Education, [1997] 1 SCR 241. Supreme Court of Canada; free to read.

Make. Entry: one decision about placement or pathway in your setting, and whose account it rested on.

Afterwards. Trace a decision about a person to the evidence it rested on and who supplied it.

Finding. No condition is shown to have failed: six bodies decided, and the Court required the decision to be made from the child's point of view, on the other evidence. The open question is the third: the reasons mention ongoing assessment without a date.

Post 5 — Three Numbers

Encounter. A court can order a plan and a date to report back. What it orders, and what it cannot, is the clearest statement there is of what law can require of a system.

Decision. Western Cape Forum for Intellectual Disability v. Government of the Republic of South Africa, 2011 (5) SA 87 (WCC). Western Cape High Court; free to read.

Make. Entry: one commitment in your setting that carries a date it is revisited, or the absence of one.

Afterwards. Find whether a commitment carries a revision date and a mechanism, or only a sentence.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the second. The first outcome in the series to bind a system to report: a sworn account within twelve months, with a door back into court.

Post 6 — Two Tables

Encounter. What a right required, what provision existed, and the distance between them stated by a body whose statement counts. The first part closes with the series' own gap statement.

Decision. International Association Autism-Europe v. France, Complaint No. 13/2002. European Committee of Social Rights; free to read.

Make. A gap statement for your setting, written from the five entries so far.

Afterwards. Write a gap statement from an audit's first entries, and say what it cannot yet show.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the third: the state's official statistics were insufficient to measure its own progress. Nothing in the decision required the state to report back.

Part 2 — Getting in

Requirements, admission, hiring. What is asked of people to enter a program, a profession or a post, and how a standard is justified when someone asks.

Post 7 — Eleven Minutes

Encounter. Every standard has a history, and the history records who it was written for. The test Canadian law applies when a standard is challenged, and what it asks the institution to show.

Decision. British Columbia (Public Service Employee Relations Commission) v. BCGSEU, [1999] 3 SCR 3. Supreme Court of Canada; free to read.

Make. Entry: one requirement in your setting that carries a number, where the number came from, and whether any document shows it is necessary to the work.

Afterwards. Separate what a program genuinely requires from what convention carried forward.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the third: the researchers' own recommendation went unanswered on the record, and relief beyond one job could not be claimed.

Post 8 — Ten Years Earlier

Encounter. A blanket rule that outranked a driving test a person had passed, and a court requiring that the person be given the chance to be assessed.

Decision. British Columbia (Superintendent of Motor Vehicles) v. British Columbia (Council of Human Rights), [1999] 3 SCR 868. Supreme Court of Canada; free to read.

Make. Entry: one blanket rule in your setting, and whether individual assessment is possible under it.

Afterwards. Find where a rule substitutes for an assessment.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the second. The restored decision ordered the discrimination to cease and one driver to be assessed; the judgment does not say what the system did next.

Post 9 — This Did Not Occur

Encounter. A residency program ended one resident's training, and the committee that heard his appeal wrote down the steps that would otherwise have been expected first.

Decision. University of British Columbia v. Kelly, 2016 BCCA 271. Court of Appeal for British Columbia; free to read.

Make. Entry: one decision that ended or narrowed someone's place in your setting, the steps your setting says it takes first, and which of them the documents show were taken.

Afterwards. Find which of a setting's own steps were taken before a decision about a person, and whether the person was in the discussion.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the second. The Tribunal ordered the university to stop and not to repeat the contravention.

Post 10 — Before Any Consultation

Encounter. A university judged one applicant the most qualified, then cancelled the recruitment over the cost of one arrangement, before any consultation on alternatives.

Decision. Sahlin v. Sweden, CRPD/C/23/D/45/2018. UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities; free to read.

Make. Entry: one accommodation your setting declined or deferred because of cost, the figure, and what was discussed with the person before the decision.

Afterwards. Find the decision record behind a declined accommodation.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the second. The Committee recommended systematic dialogue, training, and a report within six months.

Post 11 — Where Eyesight is Unnecessary

Encounter. A candidate passed the audition. Enrolment needed a medical certificate, and the academy that required it could also decide what it meant.

Decision. Çam v. Turkey, no. 51500/08. European Court of Human Rights; free to read.

Make. Entry: one entry requirement in your setting that depends on someone else's sign-off, and who decides whether the sign-off is good enough.

Afterwards. Find who holds the definition of a requirement.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the third: since 1976 the academy had made no attempt to adapt its teaching. The Court's English extracts do not show what it ordered.

Post 12 — Only in the Mind of Other People

Encounter. Employers refused or ended employment over medical findings that put no limit on the work, and a court asked where the limit was. The second part closes with the tally so far.

Decision. Quebec (Commission des droits de la personne et des droits de la jeunesse) v. Montréal (City); v. Boisbriand (City), 2000 SCC 27. Supreme Court of Canada; free to read.

Make. Interim tally: twelve entries from your setting, which condition each named, and what would count against each.

Afterwards. State an interim finding and its counter-evidence.

Finding. The condition that could not act was the first: the floor's reach was in doubt until the Court gave guidelines. Two cases went back for the employers to justify their decisions.
