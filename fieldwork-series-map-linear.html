Fieldwork — the series map

Linear, accessible version. Fieldwork is a research method for inclusive learning design, practised in public: twenty-four posts, one every two weeks, in four phases.

How to read this

This is the plain-text, screen-reader and keyboard-friendly version of the interactive series map. Nothing is hidden and nothing needs to be opened: every post appears in full, in order. Use your screen reader's heading navigation to move between phases (heading level 2) and posts (heading level 3).

Each post names three things:

Encounter
What the post opens on, and the claim it makes.
Make
The page this post adds to the working file.
Afterwards
What can be done in a setting of one's own once the page is made.

Phase 1 — Foundations

Find where a right declared and a right delivered have come apart, for one population in one setting, and draw a plan that can be corrected by what it finds.

Post 1 — The right on the wall

Encounter. A policy that was meant, and a learner it did not reach. A right declared and a right delivered are two documents, and the distance between them is where inclusion is decided.

Make. A gap map: the population named in its setting, one policy, what it says, what the record shows was delivered, and the gap between them, dated.

Afterwards. Locate a policy–practice gap for a named population before locating it anywhere else, from documents a stranger could open.

Post 2 — Who set the floor

Encounter. Three questions the hypothesis asks of any environment. Accessibility asks what the floor is. Disability justice asks who set it. Emergent learning asks whether it can move. Only the first can be answered once.

Make. A pillar audit: for each pillar, the document that shows it able to act, or the blank where that document should be.

Afterwards. Tell, from an institution's own record, which of the three pillars is not able to act.

Post 3 — Where the road runs out

Encounter. A system can change a rule and never say what the rule was resting on. Knowledge travels only as far as the systems that carry it, and every system has a file for the people it was not built for.

Make. A delivery-system scan: what the system writes down, which entries carry a number, when each was set, the assumption under each, and where the side file is.

Afterwards. Find the assumption a rule was resting on, and the place where the system recorded who it was not built for.

Post 4 — The generous blur

Encounter. A question that names everyone names no one. The care that started this is real and vast, and it has to be narrowed until one person could recognise themselves in it.

Make. A one-sentence question naming the population, the setting and the phenomenon, and the version of it one person from that population broke.

Afterwards. Write a question specific enough to answer, and hand it to someone it names before it is fixed.

Post 5 — What you can carry the whole way

Encounter. A signature is a photograph of one instant, and the study it belongs to runs for months. A study that cannot be finished is a promise made to the people most used to being dropped.

Make. A feasibility and consent sheet: time, money, access, who checks the work, the consent position as a dated statement, and which governance route applies.

Afterwards. Scope an inquiry that can be carried to the end, and write its consent position before anything is gathered.

Post 6 — A plan allowed to learn

Encounter. A proposal everyone admired, and a finding three weeks in that it had left no room for. A plan is trustworthy when a reader can tell what it promised and see where reality revised it.

Make. A one-page blueprint: objectives, method, the population, the consent position, success criteria a stranger could apply, and the date it is next revised. The working file is complete.

Afterwards. Hold a six-page working file for one population in one setting, and say what the next phase takes it into.

Phase 2 — The question in the literature

Take the question into what has already been asked, found and written about this population, and come back with a gap statement.

Post 7 — What the law requires, and what it cannot

Encounter. The law can require a floor, a process and a review date. It cannot require a system to keep learning from the people it serves. Fieldwork begins where the requirement stops.

Make. A rights map: each instrument, the obligation it creates for this population and setting, and what it leaves out.

Afterwards. Read an institution's policy against the rights instruments that bind it, and name what those instruments do not reach.

Post 8 — Who asked before

Encounter. Every population has been studied. The question is by whom, from where, and whether the people studied were ever asked what the question should be.

Make. A literature ledger: prior research on this population and setting, with the evidence type and the route for each source.

Afterwards. Find and record what has already been found about a population, with the type and reachability of every source.

Post 9 — Knowledge already on record

Encounter. Disabled people's own published accounts are evidence, not colour. They are also, often, the only record of what a policy felt like from underneath.

Make. An account register: first-person published accounts from this population, with what each establishes and its route.

Afterwards. Treat first-person published accounts as evidence, at the scope each one supports.

Post 10 — The institution's own archive

Encounter. A policy's history is a record of who it was written for. Every revision had a reason, and the reason is usually in the minutes.

Make. A policy timeline: each version of the policy, its date, and the decision record behind it.

Afterwards. Trace a policy's revisions to the decisions that made them, from the institution's published record.

Post 11 — Weighing what you found

Encounter. A source is only as strong as the route by which it can be checked. The series weighs its own sources the same way, in front of the reader.

Make. An evidence ladder, filled: every source in the file placed by type, scope and route.

Afterwards. Rank sources by what each can establish and by whether a reader without institutional access could check it.

Post 12 — The gap statement

Encounter. What is known, what is not, and what only the room can supply. The question, revised, goes into Phase 3 knowing exactly what it is asking for.

Make. A gap statement and the revised question.

Afterwards. State the gap between what the literature establishes and what the question needs, and revise the question to it.

Phase 3 — In the room

Gather evidence with the learners the question names. Paced by the practice: written as the method is applied in a real setting, with permission.

Post 13 — Before anything is gathered

Encounter. Consent is written down before the first conversation, and the governance route is chosen in the open.

Make. Agreements: the consent position in force, the governance route, and who can stop the work.

Afterwards. Put an inquiry's consent and governance in writing before it enters the room.

Post 14 — The co-design relationship

Encounter. Who is in the room, on what terms, with what recognition. Findings gathered on a population's behalf, without them, are not Fieldwork findings.

Make. Terms of the relationship: who is a co-researcher, what they decide, and what they are given for it.

Afterwards. Set up a co-design relationship in which the people the question names hold decision authority.

Post 15 — Listening

Encounter. What people say when the question is theirs.

Make. A listening record.

Afterwards. Gather accounts with learners in a form they have agreed to and can correct.

Post 16 — Counting

Encounter. What the institution's numbers show, and what they were never built to see.

Make. A counting record: which numbers exist, what each was collected for, and what the file cannot count.

Afterwards. Use an institution's own numbers at the scope they support, and say what they cannot show.

Post 17 — Who checks the work

Encounter. A second reader on the record, and the learners' own verification.

Make. A check record: what the second reader found, and what the learners corrected.

Afterwards. Build verification into an inquiry so that the people it names can correct its record.

Post 18 — The findings session

Encounter. The learners hear it first.

Make. Findings, as heard: what was presented, what was said back, what changed.

Afterwards. Return preliminary findings to the people they concern before anyone else, and record what they change.

Phase 4 — Giving it back

Say which pillar could not act, design what would let it, test it with the learners, and leave a record the institution can keep acting on.

Post 19 — Analysis, together

Encounter. What the record says when the people it names read it.

Make. A joint analysis.

Afterwards. Analyse evidence with co-researchers rather than about them.

Post 20 — The diagnosis

Encounter. Which pillar could not act, by the indicators, for this population.

Make. A diagnosis: the pillar, the evidence, and what would count against it.

Afterwards. Make a diagnosis from the indicators that a stranger could check and that states its own counter-evidence.

Post 21 — The change

Encounter. What would let the pillar act.

Make. A change design.

Afterwards. Design a change aimed at the pillar the diagnosis named, not at the symptom.

Post 22 — Testing the change

Encounter. With the learners named in the question.

Make. A test record.

Afterwards. Test a change with the people it is for, and record what they report.

Post 23 — The record

Encounter. Written so governance can read it, and so the pillar keeps acting after the inquiry ends.

Make. An institutional record: the finding, the change, the revision date, and the mechanism by which it will be revisited.

Afterwards. Write a record an institution's governance can act on, with a date and a mechanism for its own revision.

Post 24 — The return

Encounter. To the institution, to the learners, and one thing back to the series.

Make. The return: what went back to each, and what the series now holds that it did not before.

Afterwards. Close an inquiry by giving it back, and hold a complete working file, a finding, and a record of how it was gathered.
