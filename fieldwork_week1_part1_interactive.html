Most learning systems can describe their accessibility commitments in policy but cannot demonstrate them in practice.

Every high-stakes environment — a university, a workplace training program, a professional certification body — has a published policy on accessibility. The policy is often well-written. It commits to equitable access, to removing barriers, to supporting every learner. And then a disabled student arrives on a Tuesday afternoon and finds the system has not been designed with them in mind.

Access Ryerson — The case

Ryerson University (now Toronto Metropolitan University) was in full technical compliance with provincial accessibility law. Accommodations existed. Policies were published. And yet roughly two-thirds of students with disabilities never disclosed at all — because the disclosure process was itself a barrier. Compliance was being met. The systems of knowledge were not working for everyone.

Notice in your own context

Where in your environment does the policy say the right things — but the lived experience tells a different story?

The environment you work in is not neutral — it was designed with particular bodies, minds, and experiences in mind, and those choices are still operating.

The policy-practice gap is not random. It traces the outline of the people whose participation was never assumed by the original designers. Disability justice asks us to stop asking what is wrong with the learner who is struggling, and start asking what is wrong with the system they are navigating.

Access Ryerson — What they did

Rather than adding more accommodation pathways, the team conducted an environmental scan — examining the systems students were navigating, not the students themselves. Critically, disabled students led the working groups. They asked: Where does the design assume a body or mind that not everyone has? This is problem identification at the structural level.

Notice in your own context

Who was not in the room when your environment was designed — and how do you see their absence in the system today?

Your context is not just where you will apply the learning — it is the primary research site, and your own curiosity is the first instrument.

This is emergent learning: knowledge that arises from genuine contact with a real environment. It cannot be imported from another context. It must be grown where you are. Your personal interest in the gap you have already noticed — the friction, the thing that does not add up — is not a distraction. It is your first data point.

Access Ryerson — What it reveals

The institution had to become the first instrument of its own inquiry. Until the team named the specific structural gap in their own high-stakes environment, inclusive design remained an aspiration. The environmental scan — grounded in personal interest and specific context — was what made the invisible visible.

Carry this forward

Where does compliance end and genuine inclusion begin in your current practice? You don't need the answer yet. You just need to locate the gap.
