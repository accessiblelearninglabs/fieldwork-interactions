Fieldwork — what you make

Every post in Fieldwork reads one decision from the public record, and then hands the reader one entry to make from a setting of their own. This page lays those entries side by side: what each one is, why the series asks for it, and why it matters for inclusive learning where you are. Open any entry to see all four.

Kept together, the entries become an audit of one setting, made the same way the series reads the record: from documents, with dates, saying what the evidence cannot show.

Twelve entries are set out in full, one for each published post. The twelve still to come are listed as the series map describes them, and will be filled in as their posts publish.

What it can't yet show

Three habits run through all of them. Work from documents, and only ones you are entitled to read. Hand the entry to someone else and keep what they say separate from what the documents show. Write down what the entry cannot show.

Part 1

What was promised

Six entries about what a setting has committed to, and what reached the people it serves.

  1. Symbol: two overlapping pages.

    From Post 1, Twenty-five Days

    What you make

    One declared document and one delivered document from a setting you know, each with its date and who produced it. Then one sentence on whom the setting consulted between the two, and one on whom it did not.

    Why the series asks for it

    Every reading in the series starts from the same pair: what a system said and what it did. Your pair is the first line of an audit that sits beside the series’ own.

    Why it matters where you are

    A commitment to include is easy to find and easy to mean. The delivered document shows what reached a learner, and the time between the two dates is where a decision was made.

    What it can't yet show

    One entry shows one distance. It does not show which of the three conditions failed, or why.

  2. Symbol: three markers standing on one floor line.

    From Post 2, 0.0025 Percent

    What you make

    For each of the three questions, the dated document that answers it, or the blank where that document should be. Then one sentence on what sat within the setting’s reach to change, and what sat outside it.

    Why the series asks for it

    These are the three conditions the series tests in every decision: what the floor is, who decided what it covers, and whether it can move. Answering them once gives your audit its frame.

    Why it matters where you are

    It separates a floor that is missing from a floor that exists and was narrowed by a decision, and both from a setting with no way to notice. An entry that cannot tell reach from constraint will be read as an argument for more money, or as an excuse.

    What it can't yet show

    One entry answers three questions about one setting on one date. It does not show whether the answer to the second or third question was ever different, or whether it could be.

  3. Symbol: a page with a check mark beside it.

    From Post 3, The Help Number Was Not In Service

    What you make

    One standard the setting has adopted, with its date and who is named as responsible for it. Then the evidence that it has been applied, with its date and who produced it, or a note that none was found and the date you looked.

    Why the series asks for it

    It separates a standard adopted from a standard applied, which is the distance the third reading measures.

    Why it matters where you are

    An accessibility standard on paper reaches no one until somebody checks a real page, form, or course against it. The entry shows whether anyone has, and when.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show that a standard was not applied. It cannot show why, or who should have decided.

  4. Symbol: three voices above one decision, the third drawn as a dotted outline.

    From Post 4, Written Consent

    What you make

    One decision the setting made about who would receive what, from documents you are entitled to read. A list of every account the decision rested on, with each author’s role and date. Then one line on whether the people the decision was about are among the authors.

    Why the series asks for it

    The second condition asks whose knowledge shaped a decision. This entry makes the answer visible as a list.

    Why it matters where you are

    Decisions about learners are often made from reports written about them. The list shows whether the learner’s own account was part of the evidence, and whether the documents say why if it was not.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show which accounts a decision rested on. It cannot show whether a missing account would have changed the decision, or whether the setting had any way to receive it.

  5. Symbol: a calendar with one day circled.

    From Post 5, Three Numbers

    What you make

    One commitment the setting has made in writing, with its date. Then the date on which it will be revisited and how: who reports, to whom, in what form. If it carries only a sentence, the entry says so, with the date you looked.

    Why the series asks for it

    The third condition asks whether a system can notice and change. A review date and a reporting line are the smallest evidence that it can.

    Why it matters where you are

    An accessibility plan with no review date depends on someone remembering it. A date and a named reporter make the commitment one a person can ask about.

    What it can't yet show

    A commitment with a date is a mechanism for learning. This entry shows whether the mechanism exists. It cannot show whether the setting has ever used one.

  6. Symbol: two blocks with a measured gap between them.

    From Post 6, Two Tables

    What you make

    One page written from the entries so far: whether the floor exists, who decided what it covers and where, and whether any mechanism would notice that the floor was wrong. Then one line on whether the setting could measure its own progress, and from what.

    Why the series asks for it

    The first part of the series closes by saying what its six readings show, what that rests on, and what it cannot reach. This is the same statement at the scale of one setting.

    Why it matters where you are

    A page in which every sentence can be checked against a document is one another person can verify for themselves, without relying on who wrote it.

    What it can't yet show

    The entries so far are about what the setting promised and whether the promise reached anyone. They say nothing about what the setting asks of people to get in.

Part 2

Getting in

Six entries about what a setting asks of people before they can enter a program, a profession, or a post.

  1. Symbol: a ruler with its markings.

    From Post 7, Eleven Minutes

    What you make

    One requirement that carries a number: a score, a time, a minimum of hours, a word count, a grade. Where the number came from, who or what was measured to set it, and when. Then two lines: whether any document shows it was tested on people it was not set from, and whether any document shows it is necessary to the work.

    Why the series asks for it

    The second part of the series reads what systems ask of people at the door. A number looks neutral, and this entry records its history.

    Why it matters where you are

    Pass marks, time limits, and minimum hours decide who gets in and who gets through. Knowing whom a number was measured on is the first step toward knowing whom it fits.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show where a number came from and who it was measured on. It cannot show that the number is wrong, or what a better one would be.

  2. Symbol: a barrier with a magnifying glass over it.

    From Post 8, Ten Years Earlier

    What you make

    One rule that applies to a whole category of people without exception: a cutoff, a prerequisite, an automatic refusal. Who wrote it, when, and what it rests on. Then two lines: whether the setting considered assessing people individually under this rule, and whether it already does so somewhere else for a requirement of the same kind.

    Why the series asks for it

    It tests whether a rule has taken the place of looking at a person, which is what the eighth reading found.

    Why it matters where you are

    Blanket prerequisites and automatic refusals are common in admissions and in course design. Where a setting already assesses people one at a time for a similar requirement, the entry puts the two practices side by side.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show whether a rule leaves room to look at a person. It cannot show what looking would have found.

  3. Symbol: a flight of steps.

    From Post 9, This Did Not Occur

    What you make

    One decision that ended or narrowed someone’s place: a dismissal, a failed placement, a required withdrawal. The steps the setting says it takes before such a decision. Then two lines: which of those steps the documents show were taken, and whether the person was part of the discussion before the decision, and where that is recorded.

    Why the series asks for it

    It reads a setting against its own stated process, in the setting’s own words.

    Why it matters where you are

    Extra time, a second attempt, added supervision, a warning: most programs list them. The entry shows whether they reached the learner before a decision was made.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show which steps were skipped. It cannot show what the person would have done with them.

  4. Symbol: a price tag with a speech mark above it.

    From Post 10, Before Any Consultation

    What you make

    One accommodation or adjustment that was declined, deferred, or never offered because of cost. The figure, who calculated it, and what single arrangement it priced. Then two lines: whether the person was asked how else the need could be met before the decision, and whether any document shows what other funding was looked for.

    Why the series asks for it

    It records what was discussed before a cost was refused, which is where the tenth reading found the gap.

    Why it matters where you are

    A figure can close a conversation before it opens. The entry shows whether the person who needed the adjustment was asked about other ways first.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show that a conversation did not happen. It cannot show what the conversation would have decided.

  5. Symbol: a form with a signature line and a pen.

    From Post 11, Where Eyesight is Unnecessary

    What you make

    One entry requirement that depends on someone else’s sign-off: a medical form, a reference, a fitness declaration, a clearance. What the form asks the signer to certify, in its exact words. Then two lines: who decides whether a completed form is good enough and whether any document says how, and whether the setting has ever sent one back.

    Why the series asks for it

    It finds who holds the definition of a requirement, and whether that definition is written anywhere.

    Why it matters where you are

    Forms and clearances stand between people and programs. Where nothing sets out what counts as good enough, the entry records who decides in practice.

    What it can't yet show

    An entry can show who holds the definition. It cannot show how they would use it.

  6. Symbol: tally marks, four struck through and one more.

    From Post 12, Only in the Mind of Other People

    What you make

    Your entries so far, set out in order. Beside each, which of the three conditions the documents showed was least able to act. Then two lines: what the entries show when read together, in one sentence that claims no more than they hold, and one document that would count against that sentence if it existed.

    Why the series asks for it

    The series keeps its own tally in the open and names what would count against it. This is the same move at the scale of one setting.

    Why it matters where you are

    A pattern across several entries says more than any one of them. Naming the document that would count against it lets someone else test the pattern.

    What it can't yet show

    A tally can show where the documents point. It cannot show what the documents never recorded.
