# The Question You Can Hand Over
### A research question shows its worth only once you hand it to others — above all, to the people it names.

*Fieldwork · Week 4 · Part 2 · Accessibility · Disability Justice · Emergent Learning*

---

You have written a question you are proud of. It sits on the page, one clean sentence, and inside your own head it sounds exactly right.

Then you read it aloud to another person — and in the half-second before they answer, you hear it differently. You hear the word that could mean three things. You hear *learners*, plural and faceless, where last week you meant one specific person. You hear how much of the sentence was holding together only because you already knew what you meant by it.

That half-second is the whole of this week. A question kept to yourself stays perfect because nothing is allowed to touch it — not a colleague, not a calendar, not the person it claims to be about. It is comfortable precisely because it is untested, and untested is another word for not yet real. Last week you did the narrowing work: you let go of *everyone* and chose one face to see first, by name. This week you take the more exposing step — you hand that question to someone who can push on it, and you let them.

"Pressure-testing" makes this sound like admin. What it asks is harder, and truer to the work: no one person holds the whole of what a question is about, however close they sit to it. **The people who live the specific barrier it names know things about it the design can't show you, and their knowledge should lead.** Handing the question over is where *nothing about us without us* stops being a value on a slide and becomes a practical decision — the people it's about get to shape the inquiry while it is still a sentence you can change, before the study sets around it.

---

**Try**

The skill this week is **pressure-testing**: deliberately giving your question to other people, and to your own real conditions, and asking them to try to break it. It matters now because a question is cheapest to change while it is still one line. Every week that follows — feasibility, methodology, data — inherits whatever you write down now. A question that is too broad to finish, or that has quietly drifted back toward the average learner, does not announce itself later; it just makes the next twenty weeks slowly harder. Testing it now, in company, catches that while it is still free to fix — and lets the people who live the exclusion you are naming leave their knowledge in the question before it hardens.

**The activity — a three-pass pressure test**

*Do the first pass alone, the second with at least one other person, the third alone again.*

1. **Draft the sentence.** Write your research question as a single sentence. It must name — by role or lived experience, not by average — the specific learner your environment excludes most reliably: the one whose Tuesday you could describe. Reach past *how do we improve completion?* to something like *what in our remote program's design decides whether a disabled adult learner with unreliable internet stays or leaves?* Put the person in the sentence, not the footnote.

2. **Run three private tests.** Ask, in order. **Can I see one person?** — if the sentence still holds "everyone," narrow it until a single face appears. **Can I carry it through my real weeks?** — with your actual time, in your actual context, with no resources you do not have; if not, it is a question to admire, not to answer. **Can it still surprise me?** — is it open enough that the answer isn't already hiding in the wording? A question you can only confirm has its answer built in.

3. **Say it out loud to someone.** Read the sentence, unedited, to at least one other person — ideally someone positioned differently in the system than you, or who shares the lived experience the question names. Reading it aloud matters here: a question that wobbles when spoken was wobbling on the page too, and you just couldn't hear it yet.

4. **Ask for the weak points.** Give your listener these four prompts and ask them to be ungenerous: *Whom does this let you see — and whom does it quietly leave out? Where would this get stuck in a real week? Is the answer already implied by how it's phrased? If the person this question is about were in the room, what would they change about it?* Write down what they say, and let the sentence take the hits without defending it. You are here to find what breaks, and where.

5. **Rewrite from what broke.** Draft the sentence again from what the pressure surfaced — narrower where it sprawled, more honest about whom it names, more open where it had already decided. Keep both versions; the distance between them is the most useful thing you will make this week.

*Time required: about 45 minutes across two sittings — 20 alone, 20 with your reader, a few to rewrite.*
*What you need: your Week 4 carry-question, a notebook, and one willing person.*
*Adapting for your context: no peer group yet? One trusted colleague works; so does an online practitioner community, or a voice memo where you play both roles honestly. The one thing you cannot skip is a perspective other than your own; what you are after is hearing the question from somewhere outside your head.*

**Working with others**

The hardest and most important part of this week can't be done at your desk: bringing your question to the people it's about, with care for their time and expertise. Disability is not one experience, and no single vantage point holds the whole of what a question needs — which is exactly why it needs more than one hand on it. You don't have to invent how to do this well. **Shaping Our Lives** — a disabled people's user-led organisation — has published practical guidance on the involvement and consultation process, written by the people most often on the receiving end of it. Their work separates real co-production from feedback collected at the end, is clear that involvement means removing the barriers that keep the least-heard people out, and treats people's expertise as something to be valued and paid for, the way any contribution to the work would be.

**Share**

A research question is one of the few things in this work that improves when other people handle it. The people who live what it names understand it in ways no one working alone can reach.

**This week's prompt:**
*Read your question aloud to one colleague and ask a single thing: "Who does this let me see — and who does it still leave out?" Sit with their answer before you defend your sentence. If you can, bring the same question to someone who lives the exclusion it names, and ask what they would change.*

---

**Reflect**

We know how to teach people to write a clean research question. We are far less sure how to teach them to keep a real person inside it — to hold the specificity and the humanity in one sentence, right through a study, without that person quietly dissolving back into a variable. The method for scoping a question can be taught; staying with the person once the work turns technical is what the field is still working out. If you can carry the person from the first sentence to the last, you will have done the part almost everyone loses.
